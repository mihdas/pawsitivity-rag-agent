## ABOUT US 
Coexist with pawsitivity was founded by Kirtana in 2012. The goal is animal welfare. We work currently in Delhi and Faridabad. In bangalore, we are building are network and starting off with limited capacity. We mainly work with street dogs and cats. We call them "community animals" instead of "stray", as stray implies that they have run away from some place. However, these animals are a fundamental part of the ecosystem and deserve to live with us in our community. We as humans need to be more cognizant of the resources that we take from nature and should learn that the earth does not belong to only us. Hence the name coexist with pawsitivity (instead of positivity, this puts a fun spin since the animals have paws). 

## WHY WE DO THIS
The government of India and respective state governments have laws that prohibit harming community animals. However, at the municipal level there is a severe lack of funding, resources, and motivation to ensure that these animals live a healthy and prosperous life. Hence, in many places, the responsibility falls on ordinary civilians to show compassion and ensure sufficient living conditions for these furry babies.


## WHAT DO WE DO  
There are 3 main pillars of our work- feeding, vaccination, sterilization. In addition we also rescue animals in dire situations (like accidents, neglect, or severe diseases), nurse them back to health and try to get them adopted. 

## WHY DO WE FEED 

Feeding community animals is not just an act of kindness- in the Indian context, it is often an important part of humane and responsible coexistence. When done thoughtfully, feeding helps reduce immediate suffering, supports the well-being of vulnerable animals, and creates a more stable relationship between dogs and the people who share their environment.

Many community dogs and cats live with uncertainty around food, weather, illness, and human hostility. Regular, sensible feeding can help prevent prolonged hunger and weakness, especially for injured, elderly, recovering, or lactating animals who are least able to fend for themselves. It should, however, be done responsibly, with attention to local sensitivities, and the larger goal of care that includes sterilisation, vaccination, and medical support where needed.

Feeding can also contribute to safer and calmer neighbourhoods. Dogs and cats that have some consistency in food and care are often less desperate to scavenge through garbage, less likely to be pushed into conflict over resources, and easier for caregivers to monitor for illness, injury, or behavioural changes. In this way, feeding becomes part of a broader community care approach rather than an isolated act.

Most importantly, feeding helps build trust and compassion in spaces where animals are often seen only as a nuisance. In India, where community dogs are part of the urban and rural landscape, ethical feeding can support more peaceful human-dog coexistence and remind us that care, when practiced responsibly, is a shared social responsibility.


## WHY ARE THE ANIMALS STERILIZED?

Sterilisation is one of the most important pillars of responsible community animal care, but it must be understood as part of a larger, humane system of caregiving rather than a one-time fix. In the Indian context, where community animals live within shared neighbourhood spaces, simply feeding animals without also thinking about sterilisation, vaccination, medical support, and long-term monitoring does not address the root causes of suffering, conflict, and repeated litters.

Sterilisation refers to a surgical procedure that prevents animals from reproducing. In female animals, this usually means spaying, which involves removal of the ovaries and generally the uterus; in male animals, it usually means neutering, which involves removal of the testicles. Beyond preventing reproduction, sterilisation can also support better health outcomes and reduce behaviours linked to mating stress, roaming, and territorial conflict.

For community animals in India, sterilisation is essential because it helps break the cycle of repeated pregnancies, vulnerable puppies/kittens, preventable deaths, and increasing stress between animals and residents. Feeding alone may ease immediate hunger, but without sterilisation, the same area may continue to see more births than the community can safely support, leaving female dogs, puppies, and caregivers under constant strain. A responsible caregiver therefore looks at the bigger picture: food, clean water, sterilisation, anti-rabies vaccination, treatment when needed, and a stable, consistent care system.

Sterilisation is not purely just “population control”. At its heart, it is about reducing suffering, improving quality of life, and creating more stable and humane coexistence between people and community animals. When combined with ethical feeding and follow-up care, it becomes a compassionate and practical step toward long-term welfare


## ADOPTION

We choose adoption when it is genuinely needed, when an animal is abandoned, injured, unsafe, unable to survive independently, or clearly better suited to life in a home than on the street. In such cases, adoption is not an act of impulse, but a careful and ethical commitment that begins long before placement and continues long after, through screening, preparation, follow-up, and accountability.

For us, adoption means accepting that this animal’s well-being remains a lifelong responsibility, not a temporary project. That is why we approach every adoption with care, honesty, and seriousness, not to “place” animals quickly, but to ensure that when they do leave our hands, they are entering homes that are safe, informed, and committed for life.
